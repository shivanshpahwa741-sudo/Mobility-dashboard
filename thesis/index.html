ScopeScope and sector coverage

Scope in one paragraph

Mobility is anything that moves from point A to point B. We split it first by involvement: the people and firms directly involved in a movement (the rider, the driver, the vehicle, the operator, the platform that sells the trip) and those not directly involved who make it possible (policy and regulation, infrastructure, vehicle and energy supply, finance and insurance, digital rails, safety response, planning).

Inside direct movement we split by what moves: people who choose to travel, goods, and animals. Animals do not choose their trip, so their transport, an estimated ₹26,500 Cr, is freight and is counted nowhere.

Passenger movement then splits by surface: road, rail, air, water and a small "other" bucket for cable, non-motorised travel and space. Road takes ₹4.18 lakh crore of ₹6.2 lakh crore in fares (67%) and carries 55% of daily trips, so it is our focus.

Road splits into public (state-run or state-contracted) and private. Private splits by purpose: for hire, own institutional use, personal use, and the informal layer that runs outside permits.

Every leaf below carries examples and, where we have them, size. The tree ends in the gaps (problems with companies already moving in), the bets (problems nobody is working on), the startups on or next to each, and the risks.

Each split uses a single rule, so every vehicle, firm or rupee sits in exactly one box: - involvement is decided by whether you are part of the trip itself - what moves is decided by who or what is carried - mode is decided by the surface - public or private is decided by who owns or contracts the service - private purpose is decided by who the vehicle serves

How demand is cut (applies across every road leaf)

These four lenses do not add new boxes; they slice the same trips. None is split out with numbers yet.

  • Where: urban or rural; metro, Tier-2 or Tier-3 city.
  • Who travels: income band; women (partly covered); elderly and people with disabilities; students.
  • Why they travel: work, school, shopping, health, leisure. This decides who pays.
  • How far: inside a city (94% of trips) or between cities (6% of trips, 55% of passenger-km).

Step 1Who is involved in moving people

Everyone in passenger transport either moves people or makes it possible for someone else to. The first group earns from the trip. The second earns from the vehicle, the road, the money or the rules around it.

Directly involved: they make the trip happen

  • Riders, who pay the fare or own the vehicle
  • Drivers and owner-drivers of autos, cabs, buses and vans
  • Operators: state bus corporations, private bus and fleet owners, school and office fleets
  • Platforms that match riders and drivers or sell tickets

Indirectly involved: they make the trip possible

  • Rule-makers: MoRTH, state transport departments and RTOs
  • Road owners: NHAI, state PWDs and city bodies
  • Vehicle makers, fuel retailers and charging and swap networks
  • Lenders and insurers, from vehicle NBFCs to IRDAI-regulated insurers
  • Digital rails: FASTag, UPI, Vahan, booking software

Moving goods and animals sits outside this report. An animal does not choose its trip, so its transport (an estimated ₹26,500 Cr) is freight and is counted nowhere here.

Step 2Road, rail, air and water

Every passenger trip happens on one of four surfaces. Road takes two-thirds of the fare money and more than half the trips. Rail and air are run or tightly held by the state or by two airlines, and water is tiny.

Step 3Why road

Indians make about 165.5 crore trips a day longer than a kilometre. Road vehicles carry 55% of them: two-wheelers 31%, buses 11%, autos 9% and cars 4%. On top of ₹4.18 lakh crore in road fares, households spend ₹17.9 lakh crore a year owning their own vehicles.

Road is also where the rules are least settled, which is where openings appear. The Centre writes the Motor Vehicles Act and the 2025 Aggregator Guidelines; states set permits, road tax and fares. A commercial cab pays 4% of its price as tax in Delhi and up to 15% in Karnataka, and electric commercial vehicles are close to tax-free in most large states. National highways have grown to 1,46,560 km, and expressways have cut journeys like Mumbai to Nagpur from 17 hours to 8.

Step 4Public and private road transport

The state owns a small slice of road transport. Even where it pays, it increasingly hires private operators per km instead of running buses itself.

Public · ₹66,000 Cr in fares

  • 58 state transport undertakings run about 1.5 lakh buses and carry about 7 crore riders a day
  • City routes ₹18,500 Cr, intercity and rural ₹47,500 Cr
  • They lose about ₹22,500 Cr in cash a year. A city state bus costs ₹88 to ₹105 a km and earns ₹44 to ₹52
  • New electric buses are bought by the state but run by private contractors at ₹45 to ₹65 a km

Private · ₹3,51,716 Cr in fares, plus personal vehicles

  • Commercial: autos, e-rickshaws, cabs, private buses, school and office fleets, and the informal layer
  • Personal: 22.5 crore two-wheelers and 4.35 crore cars, costing households ₹17.9 lakh crore a year
  • A private AC sleeper costs ₹42 to ₹48 a km and earns ₹48 to ₹56, a 12 to 15% margin
  • The gap with the state is mostly staff: 4.4 to 6.5 employees per state bus against 2.1 to 2.4 drivers for a private contractor

Step 6The gaps nobody has closed

Each problem below is reported across sources and shows up in what people actually do. Some have funded companies working near them, but none is solved. The tag shows how far anyone has got: Unaddressed means no funded company is working on it directly; Partly addressed means startups are in but most of the problem is still open.

Unaddressed

1. Nobody organises the auto stand

Problem
98% of auto trips never touch an app. Apps try to pull rides online; nobody serves the stand, the shared route and the cash fare as they already work.
Unaddressed

2. Informal para-transit runs outside the system

Problem
Jugaads, chhakdas, white-plate vans and unregistered e-rickshaws carry rural India with no permit, insurance or credit. States respond with crackdowns, not a path to legality.
Unaddressed

3. Women's first and last mile

Problem
77% of women say poor access to transit keeps them off it. When Karnataka made buses free, women's daily trips rose from 37.5 to 67 lakh, which shows how much demand is held back.
Unaddressed

4. School vans and buses outside big cities

Problem
3.5 lakh vans are run by owner-drivers hired directly by parents, and new rules now require GPS, CCTV and regulated fares. Only 11 Indian school transport startups have raised money, and two have shut.
Unaddressed

5. 400+ cities with no organised buses

Problem
Funded bus companies chase big-city contracts inside the payment fund. Small cities can't pay reliably, so every missing bus trip becomes an auto ride or a scooter.
Unaddressed

6. One bundle of services for the driver

Problem
Drivers buy finance, insurance, charging and bookings separately. Only 19% of women auto drivers in one survey have insurance, and 93% carry loans.
Partly addressed

7. Expensive credit for e-autos

Problem
Three in four e-autos are bought on credit at 14 to 30%, and the loan or rent takes 24% of what an auto earns.
Partly addressed

8. Battery cost and life

Problem
The battery is most of an EV's cost and nobody knows its value after five summers. Swapping exists in big cities only.
Partly addressed

9. Intercity buses and cabs still sold offline

Problem
81% of private bus tickets and 70% of outstation cab trips are booked offline through agents.
Partly addressed

10. Electric cabs that make money

Problem
BluSmart lost ₹470 Cr on ₹172 Cr revenue before collapsing in 2025. No fleet has shown a profitable model.
Partly addressed

11. Crashes on two wheels and in fleets

Problem
Road crashes killed 1.83 lakh people in 2025; two-wheeler riders were 46% of them. Fleet safety tech exists for trucks and large fleets, not for scooters or small operators.

The pattern is clear. Every gap with money behind it involves a vehicle a company can own, finance or track. The six unaddressed gaps involve people and places: the stand, the van, the small town, the woman walking to the bus. That is where the next company has room.

RisksWhat could make this wrong

  • Battery value. Credit and battery gaps rest on what an EV battery is worth after years of heat.
  • State payment. Any business paid by states depends on the payment fund or suffers 2 to 6 month delays.
  • Regulation. Bike taxis and e-rickshaws are legal state by state, and crackdowns can wipe out informal supply overnight.
  • Price sensitivity. The fastest growth is in the cheapest rides, where margins are thinnest.
  • Data. Informal vehicles have no reliable count, and Tracxn's own pages sometimes disagree on funding.
