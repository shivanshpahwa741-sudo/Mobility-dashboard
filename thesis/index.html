India's passenger mobility: where the money goes, and where the openings are

Indians spend ₹6.2 lakh crore a year on fares, yet passenger transport companies as a group lose money. The profits that hold up sit with drivers and with firms that organise other people's vehicles. This report maps the sector, separates the gaps companies are already moving into from the bets nobody has taken, and lists what still needs to be proved.

November 2026 · Figures are FY26 unless stated · ₹ Cr = crore rupees

Executive summaryThe short version

Key findings

  1. Most of the market isn't a market for companies. Seven in ten trips are on a scooter, on foot or by bicycle. Of ₹6.2 lakh crore in fares, a third goes through largely informal hands and a quarter to state operators that lose money.
  2. The single largest pool is offline autos, ₹1.68 lakh crore, more than four times all ride-hailing. Drivers keep ₹75,600 Cr a year, and their biggest cost after fuel is the loan on the vehicle.
  3. Operators lose and organisers earn. Companies that run vehicles lose about ₹11,629 Cr net. Booking and commute firms that own no vehicles earn ₹3,481 Cr at a 21% margin.
  4. Platforms now sell to drivers. Daily passes replaced commission for autos, so the driver is the paying customer.
  5. Six gaps already have companies moving in, led by e-auto credit, battery swapping and premium intercity buses. These are proven but competitive.
  6. Five bets have no visible player, led by organising autos at the stand and a safe first and last mile for women. These are larger if right, but the payer is unproven.

How gaps and bets are defined here. A gap is a problem most sources report and that a startup or established company is already moving into. A bet is a problem that sources report but where no company is visibly working at scale. The two lists are mutually exclusive by construction: each problem sits in one list only.

Section 1Scope and method

Passenger mobility here means anything that carries a living being from A to B. Goods transport is a separate thesis, and moving live animals (about ₹26,500 Cr) counts as freight. Exotic travel such as space tourism and cruises is excluded. The focus is how India moves in masses every day.

The market is split three ways, which together cover all spending on getting around without overlap:

  • Intracity fares: trips inside a city or town, paid to someone else.
  • Intercity fares: trips between cities, paid to someone else.
  • Outside fares: the cost of owning your own vehicle, plus specialised pools such as ambulances, ferries and tourism.

Each segment is then tagged by who controls the money: the state, organised private firms, or largely informal operators. Numbers are reconciled across government releases, company filings and industry reports listed under Sources.

Section 2Demand: how India actually moves

Indians make about 165.5 crore trips a day longer than a kilometre. Ninety-four in a hundred stay inside a city or town. The six that leave are long, so they carry 55% of the 6,410 billion passenger-km travelled each year.

By trips, the two-wheeler carries 31%, walking 30%, cycling 12%, buses 11%, autos 9% and private cars 4%, with metro, rail and app cabs at about 1% each. The cost of riding your own scooter is therefore the price every paid service has to beat.

Demand is growing at both ends. About 80 to 85 million households should earn ₹10 to 30 lakh a year by 2031, and they buy comfort. At the same time, bikes and autos are 78% of app rides, and in small towns 90% of app trips cost ₹25 to ₹70.

Section 3Market size and segments

Fares total ₹6,20,066 Cr (about $70 billion). They were ₹4.54 lakh crore in FY20, fell to ₹3.75 lakh crore in FY22, and should reach ₹9.22 lakh crore by FY30, about 10.4% a year. Intracity is ₹3.2 lakh crore and intercity ₹3.0 lakh crore; intercity grows faster (11.3% against 9.6%), so the two are level by FY30. Owning private vehicles costs households a further ₹17.9 lakh crore, 2.9 times all fares.

3.3 Outside fares

Private vehicle ownership costs ₹17.9 lakh crore a year, of which about ₹1.25 lakh crore overlaps with commercial use. Used vehicles add ₹4 lakh crore of resale. Specialised pools total about ₹50,930 Cr, including ambulances (₹32,500 Cr), ferries and water metros (₹1,450 Cr) and shared bikes and two-wheeler rentals (₹1,180 Cr). Tourism and pilgrimage transport is not sized until the school van question in Section 9 is settled.

Section 4Who makes money

Taken together, companies that move passengers lose about ₹11,629 Cr at the operating level, and state transit alone loses about ₹26,650 Cr. The losses sit with operators. The steady private profit sits with firms that pool scattered supply and own few vehicles.

The largest pile of money belongs to no company: auto drivers take home ₹75,600 Cr a year. Their second-largest cost is the vehicle loan or daily rent.

Electric is cheaper per km in every class. Over five years an e-auto costs ₹3.29 a km against ₹5.51 for CNG, leaving the driver ₹8,965 a month better off; an EV cab costs ₹6.00 a km against ₹7.58. Lenders that track the vehicle keep bad loans at 1.5 to 2.5%, against 3.5 to 5.5% for others.

Capital has followed the platforms. Outside investors put $1.15 billion into the sector from FY23 to November 2026 across 86 deals: ride-hailing 52%, EV fleets and shared bikes 33%, bus and commute tech 15%. Rapido is valued at $3 billion, while Ola has been marked down from $7.3 billion to about $1.5 billion. Listed booking platforms trade far above operators: ixigo at 66x EBITDA and MakeMyTrip at 31x, against 17x for IndiGo.

Section 6Gaps: problems companies are already moving into

Each gap below is a problem reported across sources where at least one company is already building. These are the safer places to invest, because the problem and a paying customer are both visible. The question for each is whether the incumbents leave room.

Gap 1

Expensive credit for e-autos

Problem
Three in four new e-autos are bought on credit at 14 to 30%. The loan or rent takes 24% of what an auto earns, eating the ₹8,965 a month an EV saves.
Who is moving in
RevFin and Mufin Green Finance lend against tracked vehicles and keep bad loans at 1.5 to 2.5%.
What is still open
Credit for drivers without a history, and pricing the battery's resale value.
Wrong if
Battery values collapse after a few summers.
Gap 2

Battery cost and life

Problem
Nobody knows what a battery is worth after five Indian summers, and the battery is most of an EV's cost.
Who is moving in
Battery Smart and SUN Mobility sell swaps and battery-as-a-service, separating the battery from the vehicle loan.
What is still open
Swap density outside big cities, and standard battery formats.
Wrong if
Fixed batteries get cheap enough that swapping stops paying.
Gap 3

Platform fees drivers can afford

Problem
Commission of 20 to 30% took about 28% of an auto driver's earnings.
Who is moving in
Rapido and Namma Yatri moved to daily passes; Uber and Ola followed for autos.
What is still open
Very little. This gap is largely closed, and the fight is now over services sold to the driver.
Wrong if
States tax daily passes like commission.
Gap 4

Premium intercity buses sold offline

Problem
Private coaches grew 24.5% and sleepers earn 12 to 15%, but 81% of bus tickets are still sold offline.
Who is moving in
redBus (70%+ of online bus tickets), ixigo for small towns, and Fresh Bus running electric coaches.
What is still open
Operator software and direct booking for thousands of regional fleets.
Wrong if
Large operators sell direct and bypass platforms.
Gap 5

Reliable office commutes

Problem
Employers spend ₹22,500 Cr moving staff and want reliability and safety, especially for late shifts.
Who is moving in
MoveInSync (34% margin) and Cityflo.
What is still open
Smaller employers and Tier-2 office clusters.
Wrong if
Hybrid work cuts office attendance for good.
Gap 6

Electric cabs after BluSmart

Problem
BluSmart collapsed in 2025 through fraud, leaving riders who wanted a reliable electric cab.
Who is moving in
Evera, Snap-E and Uber Black picked up its riders.
What is still open
A fleet model that makes money; BluSmart lost ₹470 Cr on ₹172 Cr revenue in FY24.
Wrong if
Fleet unit economics stay negative at scale.

Section 7Bets: problems nobody is solving

Each bet below is a problem reported across sources where no company in our data is working at scale. They are bigger if right, but each needs a payer to show up. "Nobody" means no player in the sources gathered for this report, so each needs a competitor scan before any investment.

Bet 1

Organise the offline auto economy where it lives

Problem
₹1.68 lakh crore and 70 lakh vehicles run almost entirely offline; 98% of auto trips never touch an app.
Why nobody has
Apps try to pull rides online. Nobody serves the stand, the shared route and the cash or UPI fare as they already work.
Who would pay
Drivers, who already pay ₹20 to ₹450 a day for passes and leases.
Wrong if
Drivers won't pay for anything that doesn't bring them rides.
Bet 2

A safe first and last mile for women

Problem
77% of women say poor access to and from transit keeps them off it. Women are 47 to 48% of riders at well-lit Bengaluru metro stations and 40 to 42% at chaotic ones. When Karnataka made buses free, women's daily trips rose from 37.5 to 67 lakh.
Why nobody has
Women-only offers such as Rapido's Bike Pink cost more and drew complaints, and women are 0.8% of drivers in one sample.
Who would pay
Employers or the state, more likely than women themselves.
Wrong if
It can't match the convenience of what exists, such as a 1 to 1.5 minute pickup.
Bet 3

School transport beyond the big cities

Problem
₹27,500 Cr of school and college buses carry 1.5 crore students, growing 8% a year, and new rules require GPS and CCTV. Much of it still runs in informal vans.
Why nobody has
Schools treat it as an operational chore, and the market is fragmented.
Who would pay
Parents, who already pay ₹1,500 to ₹2,200 a month.
Wrong if
Schools keep it informal and rules go unenforced.
Bet 4

Buses for the 400+ cities with none

Problem
India runs a tenth of the city buses it needs, and 5.8 lakh buses retire by FY30. Smaller-city metros carry only 10 to 31% of planned riders.
Why nobody has
State operators lose money on every route, and states outside the payment fund pay contractors 2 to 6 months late.
Who would pay
States, per km, where payment is guaranteed.
Wrong if
Contracts sit outside the central payment fund.
Bet 5

One bundle of services for the driver

Problem
Drivers buy finance, insurance, charging and bookings from different places. Only 19% of women auto drivers in one survey have insurance, and 93% carry loans.
Why nobody has
Each company serves one piece: lenders lend, apps book, swappers swap.
Who would pay
Drivers, with platforms pushed by gig welfare laws to cover them.
Wrong if
The pieces stay cheaper bought separately.

Section 8Risks

  • Battery value. Several gaps and bets rest on what an EV battery is worth after years of heat.
  • State payment. Any business paid by states depends on the payment fund or suffers 2 to 6 month delays.
  • Regulation. Bike taxis are legal state by state, and gig welfare levies could extend to daily passes.
  • Safety. Road crashes killed 1.83 lakh people in 2025; two-wheeler riders are 46% of deaths and pedestrians 21%. Buses carry 11% of trips and 2% of deaths.
  • Price sensitivity. The fastest growth is in the cheapest rides, where margins are thinnest.
